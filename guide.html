    Shootboard user guide

    This app was written by one creator, starting from their own problems. Every module came out of something that actually went wrong. This guide walks through those problems and what was built for them — so you can tell which parts you need.

    You do not have to read it end to end. The first ten minutes is enough to start working; the rest is there when you need it.

    Step 1

    What Shootboard is, and who for

    You do one shoot and ten separate posts come out of it. A Reel, a Short, a carousel, a story, a text post. Each has its own caption, cover text, time and platform. Most people track that across a spreadsheet, a notes app and three different chats. Shootboard exists to pull that mess into one place.

    It is a content calendar, with one difference from an ordinary calendar: it also holds the production work behind the post. Is the script written, is it filmed, is the edit done, is permission cleared — all on the same screen.

    Who it helps

    • The solo creator. Shooting, editing and posting are all the same person. This is the main user.
    • Anyone filming on location. Documentary, travel, history, food — anyone who needs the address, the permission and the "watch out for this here" in one place.
    • A small team. There is a read-only link for showing the plan to an editor or a client.

    What it is NOT

    Shootboard does not post for you. It does not connect to Instagram or YouTube and it never publishes anything on your behalf. You publish; Shootboard remembers what goes out when, and what has to happen first. That is a deliberate choice: tools built on platform APIs stop working the day a platform changes its rules.

    Step 2

    The first ten minutes

    It works without an account — everything sits in your browser. But if you sign in, the same plan is on your computer, your phone and your tablet at once, and a copy lives in the cloud. For a trial, sign in.

    1. Open a project. On the Projects page, "+ Create project". A project is the name of a shoot or a job: "Kariye Mosque episode", "October product launch".
    2. Say where you are filming. You can pick a place in the same window, and add a new one from there too.
    3. Write two ideas. The Ideas page is where you throw whatever comes to mind. It does not have to be a sentence.
    4. Put one entry on the calendar. Tap a day. Mark what it is (Reel, Short, carousel…) and where it goes (YouTube, Instagram…) separately.
    5. Tick the steps. Each project has seven; tick them as you go. That is the only way the screen stays readable at a glance.

    That is enough to start. The sections below cover what you will need as the work grows.

    Step 3

    Projects and the seven steps

    A project is the job your posts come out of: a shoot, a brand job, a series. Every project has seven steps, and the list is deliberately fixed — you are not asked to design your own, because a fixed list can be read at a glance and everyone's own list cannot.

    The seven steps, ticked in order

    Script Filmed Audio Edited Approved Package Published

    Reading a row

    On a computer each project is one row, and every row is the same height. Under the name there are four small marks, all of them clickable:

    • Map — opens the location on a map. The full address is in the mark's tooltip; it is not written out, so it cannot stretch the row.
    • Bulb — this project's ideas, with the count. Clicking it opens the Ideas page filtered to that project.
    • Document — the same for scripts.
    • Next to them: type · shoot date · how many entries.

    Deadlines

    Each step can carry its own date. Tap the small calendar mark next to the tick box and you enter all seven in one window; once a date is set, the date itself takes the mark's place. A date that passes while its step is still unticked turns red on its own, and the strip at the top of the page tells you how many have slipped. This is the answer to "what did I forget".

    On a phone

    On a phone you get cards instead of a table: one card per project, the seven steps below it in a grid, each with its name. Tapping a chip ticks the step.

    Tip

    The filter chips at the top of the Projects page ("In progress", "Script ready", "Overdue") tell you the state of things in one look. Once you have forty projects, this is where you will live.

    Step 4

    Places

    If you film on location, you go back to the same spot. The Places page is for recording it once: address, district, city, map link, who gives permission and how long it takes, and "things to watch out for here" (no tripods inside, power only in the courtyard, crowded before noon).

    When a project is linked to a place, that information shows in the project window — it is not copied, it is read live. Update the note on the place and every project there sees the current version.

    The picture on the card

    You recognise a place by its picture before its name. The top of a place card shows, in order:

    • A photo — if you pasted a link, or one was found by search.
    • A map tile — if there is no photo, drawn from the coordinates in the map link. It uses OpenStreetMap: no key, no account, no cost.
    • Initials — if neither exists, in a colour derived from the name.

    So the card is never empty. Someone adding a home studio still sees a full page.

    If no map appears

    Google Maps short links (the maps.app.goo.gl kind) carry no coordinates. Paste the long link from your browser's address bar and the map shows up.

    Places without a photo

    "Look for missing photos" at the top of the page searches Wikipedia for every place that has no photo, one at a time. It usually finds historic buildings; it will not find your home studio — that is normal, and the card still shows its map.

    Step 5

    Ideas

    An idea is anything that is not a script yet: a sentence, a question, a scene. The Ideas page is where you throw them. It does not have to be tidy, because ideas do not arrive tidy.

    Merging and splitting

    Drag one idea onto another and they merge; you can pull a single part back out of a merged card. Order changes, nothing is lost.

    One idea splits into two

    Byzantine water routes What do the cisterns look like inside?

    More than one project

    An idea can belong to several projects. If "Byzantine water routes" serves both the cistern episode and the aqueduct episode, you do not copy it: add the second project with the + on the card, and cut a link with the × on a chip.

    The filter at the top works the same way: add several projects and see three projects' ideas together.

    Step 6

    Scripts

    A script is its own record, not a field on the project. The reason is practical: the project row goes to the cloud whole on every deadline change, and if the script lived inside it, an old copy on your phone could overwrite what you wrote on your desktop.

    The script window, six steps

    1. Which ideas? Every idea, searchable, multi-select.
    2. Which projects? Multi-select again — one script can serve several.
    3. Title. With an "AI writes it" button that suggests a title from the ideas you picked.
    4. Drive. Pull text in from Google Drive, or push your text back to it.
    5. What should the AI write? A roomy brief box. Ctrl/Cmd+Enter runs it.
    6. The script itself. Bold, headings and lists; a preview button shows how the marks render.

    The moment there is text in the box, the project's "Script" step ticks itself.

    Step 7

    AI: Gemini's free tier

    The AI in Shootboard runs on your own Google Gemini key. There is no AI subscription behind the app; the request goes straight from your browser to Google. Three consequences, all in your favour:

    • The cost is yours, not ours. Stay inside Google's free tier and you pay nothing.
    • Your text does not pass through us. Your script never touches a Shootboard server.
    • The limit is your limit. You decide how much you use.

    Getting a key

    1. Sign in to Google AI Studio with your Google account.
    2. "Create API key". You get a string starting with AIza… or AQ.….
    3. In Shootboard, open AI (Gemini) from the left rail, paste the key, hit "Test the connection", save.

    How far does the free tier go?

    Google's free tier gives you a certain number of requests per minute and per day. Those numbers change from time to time; the current ones are on Google's pricing page. Someone writing a few scripts and a few titles a day stays well under them. If you do hit a limit, the app says so plainly — waiting a minute and trying again is enough.

    Where the key lives

    By default, only on the device you typed it into, in that browser's storage. If you do not want to be asked again on your phone, tick "Keep this key in my account" in the AI window: it is written to your own account row (only you can read it) and your other devices pull it down at sign-in. That box is off by default — a key is a tap on your wallet, and it is not copied anywhere you did not ask for. "Remove key" deletes the account copy too.

    Writing a title with AI

    Title

    Under Byzantium: the cisterns beneath the city

    ✨ Write with AI

    While the AI works the button locks and counts the seconds — so "is it working or is it stuck" never comes up. It will not write over text you already have without asking. Afterwards, "undo" brings your old text back.

    The AI does not know what it wrote

    Gemini only knows what we send in that one request. Dates, names and numbers are yours to check. The rule that holds across this whole guide: the AI writes a draft, you decide what gets published.

    Step 8

    Calendar and entries

    An entry is a single post. Every entry carries two independent labels: what it is (Reel, Short, video, story, carousel, text post, poll) and where it goes (YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, Facebook, Threads, X, Pinterest, LinkedIn). They are separate on purpose: the same shoot goes out as a Reel on Instagram and a Short on YouTube.

    The same plan has month, week, day and table views. Press and hold an entry to drag it to another day — with a mouse or a finger.

    An entry is dragged to another day

    MON TUE WED THU FRI

    The "Posted / Scheduled" tick

    You tick this after you publish. Shootboard does not listen to the platforms; we say so plainly, because this is exactly where a wrong expectation goes off.

    Time zones

    Every entry carries its own time zone, so a post planned for another country makes it clear which clock the time refers to. The calendar marks it too.

    Step 9

    Caption templates

    The same two lines you put under every post — the subscribe line, the links, the signature — so you do not retype them. The model in one sentence: one general text, and optionally per-platform exceptions.

    • The general text goes into every caption.
    • If you wrote a special text for one platform, only that platform gets it; the rest keep taking the general one.

    You enter your handles once (@yourname) and the template uses them.

    Step 10

    Reminders and calendar subscription

    If you have entered deadlines, Shootboard can warn you. Two channels:

    • Browser notification. When you open the app, today's and overdue steps come through. That is why it asks for permission.
    • Calendar subscription (.ics). Adds your deadlines to Google Calendar, Apple Calendar or Outlook as a subscription. This is the stronger one: your phone's calendar reminds you even when Shootboard is closed.

    The subscription address is a random token long enough not to be guessed; hit "refresh" and the old address dies and a new one is born.

    You choose which steps you want warning about: the day before, on the day, once overdue.

    Step 11

    Importing from a spreadsheet

    If you work in a spreadsheet today, you should be able to move without emptying your hands. "Import" in the footer understands text copied out of CSV, Google Sheets, Excel and Notion.

    1. Copy the rows, header row included.
    2. Paste into the import window.
    3. Shootboard guesses the columns (date, time, platform, title, type, project, posted) — correct it by hand where the guess is wrong.
    4. The preview shows what is about to come in. Then you confirm.

    It reads most date formats: 2026-09-10, 10.09.2026, 22 September 2026, September 10, 2026. If you gave a project name and a project by that name exists, the entry is linked to it.

    Step 12

    The shareable link

    To show an editor or a client what is going out this week, you do not need a screenshot. You can generate a read-only link. Whoever opens it only reads: no account, nothing to change.

    The link carries the calendar only: date, time, type, platform, title, whether it went out, and the project name. Captions, hashtags and AI briefs are deliberately left out — those are your working notes.

    Close the link and the file is deleted; the address dies with it.

    Step 13

    Backups and your data

    Your work sits in three places, in this order on purpose:

    1. Your browser. Every change is written here first. It works with no internet.
    2. The cloud (Supabase). If you are signed in, changes go up; your other devices read them down.
    3. An automatic backup. Once a day, when you open the app, a copy of everything is written to your own folder. The last seven days are kept. You do not have to do anything. The account window lists the days; tap one to restore it.

    There is a manual backup too: "Export Backup" in the footer downloads everything as one file — calendar, projects, places, ideas, scripts, templates. "Restore Backup" reads it back and merges: what is in the file is added, where a record already exists the file wins, and nothing you have now is deleted. Open an old backup by mistake and you do not lose today's work.

    How often should you take a manual backup?

    The automatic one runs daily and covers most of it. Monthly is enough by hand: that file is a copy independent of the project, so it survives even if something goes badly wrong on the cloud side.

    Step 14

    Account, privacy, deletion

    The only reason to have an account is so your work moves between devices. Two ways in: Google in one tap, or email and a password. Forget the password and you ask for a sign-in link — nobody gets locked out.

    The Your account window in the left rail holds: sign out, change password, the list of automatic backups, the export, and delete your account.

    Deleting cannot be undone, so the button stays disabled until you type your own email address. When you do, your calendar, projects, ideas, scripts, places, settings and automatic backups are deleted from the server.

    The details are in privacy and terms of use.

    Step 15

    Frequently asked

    Does Shootboard post for me?
    No. It does not connect to the platforms and never publishes on your behalf. It remembers what goes out when; you publish.
    Is it free?
    Nothing is charged during the trial. For AI you use your own Google Gemini key; stay inside Google's free tier and that costs nothing either.
    Will I pay extra for the AI?
    Google's free tier is more than enough for a few scripts and titles a day. Go past it and Google's pricing applies; Shootboard does not put a fee in between.
    Where do my scripts live?
    In your browser, and in your own account row if you are signed in. When you use AI the text goes straight from your browser to Google; it never touches a Shootboard server.
    Does it work offline?
    Yes. Every change is written to your browser first and sent up when the connection returns.
    Can I use it on a phone?
    Yes, and it has its own phone layout: tabs fixed at the bottom, the project table turns into cards, windows open from the bottom. Add it to your home screen and it behaves like an app.
    Can I take my data elsewhere?
    Yes. "Export Backup" downloads everything as a single JSON file. There is no lock-in.
    Can we use it as a team?
    Not fully yet. There is a read-only link for showing the plan; roles and shared editing are the next step.

    Something missing, something unclear? The Feedback button in the app goes straight to the person who writes it.
